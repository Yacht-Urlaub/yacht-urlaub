A Taste of Paradise: Sailing the Caribbean

From Grenada to St. Vincent and the Grenadines

Arrival and First Day Aboard

We fly from Vienna to the Caribbean via London, with an overnight layover, and touch down right on schedule in Grenada. Excitement is running high as the taxi carries us to the nearby marina, where the catamaran we've chartered through Yacht-Urlaub is waiting for us.

Part of the crew heads off to stock up on provisions while the rest of us help the skipper haul the luggage aboard and get it stowed away. We could have ordered groceries ahead of time, but we wanted to see what was available locally and pick things out ourselves. Before long, the whole crew is back together, loading up everything we'll need for the days ahead. It's March, and unlike back home in Austria, it's pleasantly warm here — we can hardly wait to cast off. Our Caribbean sailing vacation is about to begin!

Our first destination is a small bay just outside Grenada Harbour, and we want to get there before dark. Since daylight lasts about twelve hours year-round this close to the equator, it's reliably dark by around 6 p.m. and light again by 6 a.m. We motor out past a lineup of impressive sailing and motor yachts and head for the open sea, looking forward to ten days of life aboard and the Caribbean waters ahead.

Our first dinner aboard — steaks off the boat's own grill — paired with a postcard-perfect sunset, is the ideal way to kick off our Caribbean getaway.

A generous shot of rum rounds out this wonderful evening, and we tumble into our berths, worn out in the best way. They're surprisingly comfortable, easily rivaling a decent queen-size bed back home. Each cabin is a perfect fit for two, with plenty of storage room as long as you don't overpack with clothes you won't need anyway. Every cabin also comes with its own head — what everyone on board calls the "wet room" — complete with a private shower, sink, and electric toilet, which makes life on board that much more comfortable.

Carriacou

After breakfast, we set off toward a small island just off Carriacou. It's postcard-pretty and quintessentially Caribbean, with white sand beaches and swaying palms. Pelicans dive straight down into the water again and again, fishing the shallows — I could watch them for hours.

Tonight we're planning dinner at a restaurant on Carriacou. Our skipper runs us in to the beach, where we make it ashore safely despite the usual surf. We have no idea yet that it's about to turn into a long, hilarious, and — in every sense of the word — wet evening.

No sooner have we settled in at one of the beautifully set tables than it starts to rain — pour, really — and it barely lets up the rest of the night. The owner is wonderfully hospitable and clearly used to the Caribbean's fast-changing weather. She also speaks great German, as it turns out, and makes us feel welcome right away. We end up squeezed in at the bar alongside four other Austrians and two Germans.

The rum punch here — the best we have on the entire trip, as it turns out — quickly makes us forget all about the rain drumming on the roof. The food is just as good: spiny lobster with rice and salad, followed by chocolate cake.

Union Island

The next day we sail on to Clifton on Union Island, our first stop in the Grenadines proper. Clifton, the island's main town, is best known for its small airport and its harbor with a ferry dock. After a stroll around town that afternoon, we duck into one of the bars — its riot of color alone is enough to lure you in for a drink. We end up swapping stories with other sailors there, and the conversation flows as easily as the rum.

In good spirits, we head over to Lambi's Bar, where we've booked a table. The menu is varied and the food is excellent. A couple of steel drum musicians keep us entertained, their nimble fingers coaxing out those unmistakably Caribbean rhythms. Thoroughly content, all six of us pile into the dinghy and putter back through the dark to our catamaran.

Early the next morning, we take the dinghy in to do some shopping. The little shops and market stalls offer up a wonderfully colorful mix of goods.

After stocking up on plenty of EC dollars — the local Eastern Caribbean currency — we mostly buy fresh fruit and vegetables for the galley. Wherever you look, the riot of color on the houses, huts, and fences makes for a scene that feels wonderfully, distinctly Caribbean.

Loaded down with our shopping bags, we head back happy to the "dinghy dock" — a nice little Clifton institution, as it happens. Our skipper is already waiting to run us back out to the catamaran, which is moored on a buoy not far offshore.

We reach our next stop in the early afternoon: a bay with a small settlement and a classic palm-fringed Caribbean beach — the perfect place to drop anchor. We've been waiting for this! Time for a swim, some snorkeling, and a little paddleboarding...

We'd actually planned to cook aboard again tonight, but a beachfront restaurant nearby quickly changes our minds. Without a reservation we probably wouldn't have gotten a table at all — plenty of hotel guests are there soaking up the same special atmosphere we are. The food turns out to be a real highlight — the local soup and the catch of the day both come highly recommended! We're having such a good time that we completely forget to take any photos, but the evening is one we won't forget. The live music playing in the background — and more than a few rum punches and Painkillers — probably had something to do with that!

A long walk down the palm-lined beach under a blanket of stars caps off what has been a truly dreamy day in the Caribbean.

St. Vincent

The next day we sail to the largest island in the Grenadines, St. Vincent, home to the capital, Kingstown. But we're headed for the island's most famous bay: Wallilabou Bay. Back in 2003, this once-obscure little bay became known around the world overnight when it served as a filming location for Pirates of the Caribbean, starring Johnny Depp. Naturally, we can't wait to see it.

Long before we reach the bay, local boat vendors come out to meet us in craft of every size — some on surfboards, others in nothing more than a hollowed-out log. They're selling everything from fish and fruit to jewelry and island tours. Before long, the water around our catamaran turns into a floating market, with vendors circling us and holding up their wares. Of course, we can't resist — we end up buying bracelets, fresh fruit, and tuna straight off the line for dinner.

The bay, we discover, is ringed by small, densely wooded hills. Various buildings line the shore, along with what look like the crumbling remains of old film sets — a hint of a more glamorous past. The water here isn't really swim-friendly, so two of us take the dinghy out to scout a better spot for snorkeling while the rest of us stay aboard and master the art of doing absolutely nothing.

Later that afternoon, everyone heads ashore to get a closer look at the leftover movie set. We wrap up the day with a fantastic dinner of grilled tuna, courtesy of our onboard "chef," and — Caribbean tradition demands it — a proper pour of rum. The sunset, too, lives up to every dream we've ever had about these islands.

We start the new day with a hearty breakfast — eggs and bacon, muesli, and some Caribbean "apples." The exotic fruit we picked up the day before turns out to be wonderfully sweet.

Before leaving the bay, we swing by for one last lap so we can get a good photo of the offshore rock formation that looks uncannily like a triumphal arch.

Bequia

Now we sail south again, bound for the town of Port Elizabeth. The crossing between islands is rough today, and while the rest of the crew is having a blast with all the wind and waves, I retreat to the salon and just enjoy the gentle rocking of the boat instead. The bay off Port Elizabeth turns out to be sizeable, and it's busy with yachts of every kind.

The water is clear and impossible to resist, so we jump right in — a swim is exactly what the afternoon calls for.

In the early evening we all take the dinghy into town and wander along the waterfront promenade, taking in all the little things there are to discover.

There are plenty of colorful shops and stalls that practically beg you to browse. A T-shirt reading "Sail fast, live slow" sums up our current lifestyle pretty perfectly — by now we've fully settled into the Grenadines' unhurried rhythm.

We stroll along the water toward a pizzeria the skipper recommended for dinner, arriving early enough to snag a good table. The view of the bay and the slowly setting sun make this spot feel genuinely special. The pizzas come in three sizes, and naturally we go for the XXL — leaving plenty leftover for our next meal aboard.

We get an early start the next morning since we've got a fairly long stretch of sailing ahead of us. A special destination is waiting for us, and it promises to be one of the real highlights of the trip. Simply Caribbean — simply beautiful.

We tie up to a mooring buoy, tucked behind a reef that shields us from the swell of the open sea. The water here is shallow and crystal clear, so clear you can see straight down to the seafloor. And that's not all — we get an up-close view of the big sea turtles swimming all around our catamaran. They surface to the left and right, in front of and behind the boat, poking their heads up for a breath two or three times before diving back down. It's a mesmerizing sight, and easily one of the most magical moments of the whole trip — one we won't soon forget.

Tonight brings another long-awaited highlight: the lobster barbecue. We'd seen a documentary about it on TV a while back and have been looking forward to this ever since. The evening includes a boat transfer, so Alphonso picks up the crew in his namesake boat while our skipper follows behind in the dinghy. We land on one of the nearby beaches and suddenly find ourselves standing on exactly the kind of dream beach you usually only see in photos or movies. We've arrived in paradise.

Rows of colorful tables, set just as colorfully, are waiting for us. As always, there's rum punch to start, followed by an array of salads, and then beautifully prepared lobster with all kinds of delicious sides.

The guests are an international crowd, and so, it turns out, is the wine — which we thoroughly enjoy. So it's probably for the best when, not long after sunset, our hosts start gently but firmly nudging everyone to head back to their boats. After all, finding the right catamaran in the dark takes some doing, and the sea isn't exactly a well-lit parking lot.

The next morning brings a mix of sun and rain. I find it fascinating watching the wind pick up as a curtain of rain rolls steadily closer. It falls fine but steady, leaving the sea a deep, dark blue in its wake. Once the sun breaks back through the clouds, the Caribbean Sea regains all its brilliance and turns emerald green again. I can't resist a swim, and since only a handful of yachts are anchored in the bay this morning, I practically have this oversized swimming pool in the middle of the Caribbean all to myself.

It's hard to put into words what it feels like to swim in this water, or to lie back in the warm sand under the palms. You just have to experience it for yourself to really understand what a Caribbean vacation dream come true feels like. Nothing is too much, nothing is missing — it's all here.

Of course, part of you wants to stay here forever, but there are still more beautiful islands waiting in the Grenadines. The landscape itself keeps surprising us with its variety — gentle rolling hills in some places, steep cliffs and jagged peaks in others, all of volcanic origin and mostly draped in lush, evergreen vegetation kept alive by regular rainfall.

That evening we treat ourselves to another excellent lobster dinner before sailing on the next day. The skipper has really nailed the route, because what's waiting for us next might just be the most beautiful bay of the whole trip. If the perfect Caribbean beach exists anywhere, it's here.

The colors are the first thing that catch my eye — countless brightly patterned sarongs hanging along a line strung up on the beach. Only a few sailboats are anchored in this little bay, so our skipper has his pick of buoys close to shore. The sea isn't blue anymore; it's pure emerald green, as though we were floating over one giant gemstone. I honestly can't tell if I'm dreaming or if this is really happening. It's simply beautiful.

When we take the dinghy ashore later, I can hardly wait to dig my toes into the sand. We walk down the beach toward the small settlement nearby. Those colorful sarongs really are eye-catching, and I can't resist picking up one of the more vibrant ones for myself.

The young woman who sells it to me is a little shy, but — like most people we've met on the islands of the Grenadines — genuinely friendly. It's not just the islanders' vibrant clothing that catches the eye, but their hairstyles too: long braids, elaborately pinned-up styles worn by both men and women, and the occasional charming hair ornament on a little girl, alongside plenty of colorful headwear everywhere you look.

A small village market lets us stock up on the essentials — a bottle of rum definitely counts as essential. We settle into a shady little spot where a friendly, easygoing young woman keeps us supplied with cold drinks and, just as importantly, the Wi-Fi password.

While the rest of the crew is happy to stay in the shade, I wander back across the village square toward the beach to watch island life unfold. Around the village, the men seem to be busy patching up boats and mending fishing nets. Some of them, along with the women, have retreated into the shade of the huts to wait out the midday heat. The kids, on the other hand, are full of energy, splashing around in and along the water — probably the smartest move at this hour. So I decide to follow their lead and go for a swim myself, soaking up every last bit of this Caribbean sailing dream.

And as the saying goes, "leave while it's still good..."!
