Mallorca – Menorca – Cabrera

A discovery tour through the northern Balearics

Day 1 – From Home to the Yacht in Just 6 Hours

Getting to Palma de Mallorca couldn't be easier — from home, through the airport, and onto the yacht in just six hours, since our starting marina, "El Arenal," is only a short taxi ride from the airport. After the usual provisioning and checking in on the yacht, it was already 5 p.m. Marina El Arenal doesn't just have a swimming pool and a cozy restaurant — it also sits right at the end of one of the most famous party strips for German-speaking tourists: the Ballermann. Not exactly the focus of this trip, but plenty of crews come to this marina specifically to check it out — so we took a look at the spectacle too. Bar after bar, entire party palaces line the strip. A fun way to kick off the first night of our two-week sailing holiday. You could easily spend hours, or days, at the Ballermann — but we were only brief guests, since the island circumnavigation was starting the next day.

Our goal: circling Mallorca and Menorca in a figure eight — a relaxed pace over 14 days.

Day 2 – The Journey Begins

Reasonably recovered from the night before, we set off on our first day at sea after a hearty breakfast, heading west in a light breeze. Our rookie Florian felt a little queasy — it was his first time on a sailing yacht, and he had no experience with the waves yet. Our skipper reassured him: "It'll pass, it's just a matter of getting used to it. Just watch the horizon a bit and relax."

After about two hours underway we reached Isla Dragonera, a small offshore island shaped like a dragon — the first scenic highlight of our trip. We tacked through the passage between Mallorca and Isla Dragonera after a swim stop. Once a bit of wind picked up, we raised the sails for the first time. Florian had settled into sea life by now, and honestly, we were all feeling great. The vacation had found its rhythm!

An hour later we reached our destination for the day, Port de sa Foradada — a bay with a beautiful rock formation offshore, somewhere along Mallorca's west coast. We christened our galley for the first time here too; dinner on board was the perfect finish to a successful first day at sea.

Later that night, our crewmate Bernhard risked a dip in the cool water, not realizing that jellyfish, which hide from the sun during the day, tend to come out more at night. He got stung on the hand, though nothing too dramatic: rinse with saltwater, scrape off the stingers with a credit card, rinse again with saltwater, then keep the area out of the sun. The tried-and-true remedy for a minor jellyfish sting.

Day 3 – Rounding the North Cape

Today brought our cruise's first real challenge: rounding Mallorca's North Cape. Guidebooks warn that this stretch can take some work, but with an experienced skipper, a fit crew, and a good yacht, we figured it wouldn't be a problem for us.

Before reaching the North Cape, though, we got to admire the spectacular scenery along Mallorca's west coast — the steep cliffs and the harbor of Port de Sóller. A few of us realized just how rare a view this really is; the pictures you can take from the water out here are stunning, though no camera can quite capture some of the more dramatic cliffs.

As we slowly closed in on the cape, we noticed, once the sails were up, that the current was running south — straight against us. Since the wind was also blowing right along the coastline, we had no choice but to tack. Our sailing skills were put to the test — everyone pitched in, steering, calling out commands, inching the yacht closer to the North Cape bit by bit.

The sea grew rougher, the wind stronger. That's when it hit us — the guidebooks were right: rounding the North Cape under sail is genuine work. But we knew that going in and had a blast working together toward our goal. In the end we made it, cleared the North Cape, and set course for our destination for the day, Port de Pollença — a popular vacation town on Mallorca's north coast with plenty of restaurants and bars. We also stocked up on water and food, getting ready for the next day's crossing to Menorca.

Day 4 – Crossing to Menorca

A little worse for wear from Port de Pollença's nightlife, and after the morning's fuel run with the motorized dinghy, we began the crossing to Menorca. The swell was moderate — a second challenge for our rookie Florian, though this one probably had more to do with the previous night's drinks than the waves. After a three-hour crossing we reached Menorca's noticeably flatter western coastline.

Cala Macarella, on the island's south coast, was our destination for the day. We reached it quickly, dropped anchor, and made our first attempt at fishing. We had some success fairly fast — though not quite enough to feed the whole crew. The restaurant in the bay made for a welcome backup, and we were slowly warming up to the good Spanish cuisine — especially a homemade garlic sauce with olives and bread, often served as a starter. Wonderfully simple, but absolutely delicious.

Day 5 – Bays as Far as the Eye Can See

Our journey continued along Menorca's southern coast — one anchorage after another, some with hotels nearby, many wonderfully secluded and perfect for yachts looking for peace and quiet. Our priority, though, was bays with restaurants nearby — our onboard fishermen might get lucky, but better safe than sorry. So we settled on Cala Canutells: a small holiday settlement with a restaurant serving excellent homemade paella.

Day 6 – Eastern Menorca

We reached the eastern end of Menorca's south coast, where the landscape starts to shift a little. With a moderate breeze blowing, we got to sail with full sails while taking turns being towed behind the yacht on a line — pure refreshment, and a bit of a workout too, since at low speed you have to work to hold on after just a short while.

Our destination for the day was the capital, Mahón (also spelled Maó), whose nearby airport draws plenty of land-based tourists too. Mahón carries a strong British influence from its past, giving it a uniquely blended culture within Spain. Sailing into Mahón's long, deep-cut harbor is a fascinating approach. The Old Town itself sits up on a rise, reachable by either elevator or stairs. We got lucky — a town festival seemed to be underway in the Old Town, with street concerts and bars setting out extra benches everywhere.

For us, this was our first town after two nights in a bay or at anchor — and a good chance to restock supplies. We enjoyed dinner at a restaurant near the harbor and wrapped up the evening at a bar before heading back on board to soak in the town's nighttime atmosphere from our floating mooring.

Days 7 to 9 – Quiet Days in Northern Menorca

Our sailing holiday continued along Menorca's northern coast, showing us some of the most striking scenery of the trip. Sparsely populated stretches of coast, secluded beaches — perfect for yachts, with plenty of bays ideal for overnighting. The wind conditions were perfect these days for pushing us further west too. At Cala Tirant, at the edge of a holiday settlement, we visited a beautiful beach and enjoyed the bay away from the tourist development — having it entirely to ourselves.

Day 10 – Western Menorca

Arriving in western Menorca, we visited the bustling Old Town of Ciutadella. Until the 18th century, Ciutadella was Menorca's capital and is rich in cultural treasures at its center. For us it made a welcome change of pace after days of quiet away from the tourist crowds. The natural harbor offered plenty of places to eat and drink, and the nightlife scene and bars here were rich and appealing too. Since we were already preparing for our crossing back to Mallorca, we went easy on the alcohol — the crossing over had already shown us the sea between the two islands can get a bit rough.

Day 11 – Crossing Back to Mallorca

The day started with a solid breakfast at a restaurant right across from our yacht, before heading back to Mallorca. We cast off right at 11, estimating about four hours for the crossing. To our surprise, the sea was very calm and pleasant. Along the way we kept spotting large floating objects and wanted to take a closer look. Our crewmate Bernhard was the obvious volunteer for the job — he jumped straight in to investigate. Turns out the big wooden buoys, decorated with greenery, are deliberately placed to be visible from a distance: they mark local fishermen's nets.

After that little research expedition, we continued on toward Cala Ratjada, which seemed like a fitting destination — our first stop back on Mallorca, promising a cozy, sheltered harbor. Arriving in Cala Ratjada, it was immediately clear: Mallorca remains a party island for our German neighbors, and Cala Ratjada is no exception, with countless bars to prove it. A small marina sits right in the middle, surrounded by hotels and excellent infrastructure for every taste. We'd also clearly landed in one of the region's fishing hubs — wonderful seafood restaurants line the harbor. It didn't take us long to decide what we'd be having for dinner.

After an excellent seafood platter — grilled lobster included — and a stroll along the harbor, we headed into Cala Ratjada's nightlife, which left us with more than one memorable impression. The contrast to our recent days on Menorca couldn't have been sharper — this town is thoroughly touristy.

Day 12 – The Other Side of the Vacation

After a somewhat longer night, we left the harbor late in the morning and set sail south. A freshening south wind kept us moving well. Today's destination was Porto Colom or one of the nearby bays. Mallorca's east coast is rich with places to stop — small fishing villages and swimming bays alike invite a lunch break. But the wind stayed in our favor, so we kept sailing south and enjoyed the late-summer afternoon sun.

That evening we anchored at Cala Marçal, near Porto Colom — a small swimming cove with a beach, a hotel behind it, and a handful of shops for supplies. We took the dinghy to the sandy beach, where the sunbathers eyed us with some curiosity — it probably isn't often that sailing tourists come ashore here. A nearby restaurant caught our attention, so we booked a table there for dinner, then finished our shopping and headed back on board to stow the provisions. A wonderful day of sailing found a fitting close with a swim and a good meal.

Day 13 – One of the Most Beautiful Spots

Our second-to-last day of sailing dawned, and with the steady light wind we raised the sails once more, heading for Cabrera. Cabrera is a small nature reserve island that was once used as a prison island. To be allowed to anchor there, we'd reserved a buoy back at the start of our trip — there's only one large bay on the entire island where yachts are permitted. By mid-afternoon we reached our destination for the day. During the docking maneuver, the steering system failed, forcing us to switch to the emergency tiller. It takes the place of the regular steering and connects directly to the rudder blade — steering takes noticeably more muscle without any mechanical advantage, but it was no problem at all for our skipper. Cabrera's singular natural beauty is best enjoyed with a walk around the island — there's a ruin to climb, and hiking trails wind through the nature reserve. Since fishing is banned here too, the marine life is remarkably tame and unbothered when you go for a swim.

Day 14 – Heading Home

Our last day aboard our yacht dawned. With the steering still out, relying on the emergency tiller and autopilot, we headed home, straight for the harbor at El Arenal. We'd achieved our goal of circling Mallorca and Menorca. It's hard to say which impressions stand out the most — there were simply too many over the past 14 days. What everyone agrees on, though, is the joy of the trip and the certainty that we'll be back to Mallorca and the Balearics for another sailing holiday.
